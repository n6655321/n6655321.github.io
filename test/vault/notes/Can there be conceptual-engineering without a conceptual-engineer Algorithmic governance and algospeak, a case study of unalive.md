# Can there be conceptual-engineering without a conceptual-engineer ? Algorithmic governance and algospeak, a case study of unalive

When a word linked to a concept becomes taboo, speakers still wanting to discuss the concept face pressure to find an appropriate lexical substitution. But the linguistic innovation they propose, in turn, might frames the concept differently and allow them to think about it differently. Can it then be said that they involuntarily engaged in different word conceptual engineering ? Or does being constrained make it a mere ineluctable conceptual change rather than true intentional conceptual engineering?

Algospeak is an algorithm-safe slang TikTok users engage in as a linguistic strategy to evade the risk of censorship. They fear that mentioning certain taboo words might trigger the algorithm and cause their content to be erased or _shadowbanned_ because of TikTok’s “algorithmic opacity” (Burrel (2016)). This expression refers to the lack of transparency around its moderation policies. Algospeak encompasses x-phemistic phrases to address prohibited concepts which “do not necessarily reflect the taboo as perceived by the creator, but rather the taboo imposed directly and indirectly by the platform through TikTok’s _Community Guidelines_, or through algorithmic folklore” ((Marlena Moskal & Natalia Supernak (2026)).
M.Moskal and N.Supernak have identified _unalive_ as a form of morphological innovation, more precisely, a derivation, to x-phemistically address the prohibited concept of death. It is used as a substitute for “to kill” and “to die”, both of these verbs being taboo on TikTok.
This raises a philosophical question : how can one single verb work as a synonym for two verbs which are not themselves synonyms?
Moreover, the apparent composition of _unalive_ suggests that it was built as the antonym of another verb, just like “undoing” was built out of “doing”, but this hypothetical primitive verb is nowhere to be found.

In the first section of this article, I argue that _unalive_ is a lexical innovation and the opportunity for a conceptual change. More precisely, this linguistic framing makes available a new conceptualization of death, as the mere undoing of something which was done in the first place. This conceptual change is due to the fact that _unalive_ make salient different information for speakers than “to kill” or “to die”, and that it might encourage speakers to revise previous moral intuitions.
In the second section of this article, I ask if this conceptual change can be considered as an instance of conceptual engineering, since its emergence is the result of an intentional strategy in a constrained context. Users coining _unalive_ while participating in algospeak would be an instance of collective and constrained conceptual engineering. This example would allow to challenge the idea that conceptual engineering is always attributable to one identifiable author, namely, the conceptual engineer and the idea that her goal is to provide a better concept.

## **I. _Unalive_ makes possible a conceptual change**
This verb is introduced as a x-phemistical substitute for the taboo terms « to kill » and « to die ».
The two verbs that _unalive_ has emerged to replace are not equivalent. Observing that it both transitive and intransitive uses will help to solve this puzzle. I hope to show that _unalive_’s lexical extension is broader than those of “to kill” and “to die”, as it encompasses both of them. This allows a different conceptualization of death
Moreover, _unalive_ is a new word whose composition is apparent, contrarily to the verbs it substitutes for, which appear to be primary forms. This morphological difference might pave the way for a semantic difference. This strengthens the first argument, as it shows that regardless of extensional equivalence, _unalive_ frames death in a new way and makes salient new information for speakers.

### **1. Its transitive ambiguity makes salient new information and allows to think of death differently**
#### **a. This verb has a broader extension because it can be used transitively and intransitively.**
_To unalive_ admits both transitive and intransitive uses, whereas “to kill” is transitive and “to die” is intransitive.

(i) As a transitive verb, it reads as “to kill”. “She unalived them” can be translated as “She killed them”. It can also be reflexive, the reflexive pronoun being sometimes eluded. Then, “to unalive (oneself)” can be translated as [[!!! Le suicide !|"to kill oneself"]].

(ii) As an intransitive verb, it reads as “to die”, and “She wants to unalive” can be translated as “She wants to die”.

_To_ _unalive_ refers both to killing, the causing of one’s death (i) and to dying, the actual process resulting in one’s death (ii). “They unalived her : she unalived” is intelligible. It can be translated as “They killed her : she was killed” or “They killed her: she died”. Conversely, “They killed her : she killed” and “They died her : she died” are unintelligible, as they don’t admit both transitive and intransitive uses. Therefore, _to unalive_ is not a mere x-phemism for “to kill” or “to die”. Its polyvalence allows it to encompass many uses. M.Moskal and N.Supernak list some of them and report that “it can function as an attributive noun in, e.g., _an unalive note_ (a suicide note), a noun, e.g., _bathtub unalive_ (a bathtub suicide), or, most commonly, a verb, in, e.g., _to unalive oneself_ (to kill oneself).”((Marlena Moskal & Natalia Supernak (2026)). _To_ _unalive_ seems to have a broader lexical extension than “to kill” or “to die”, despite encompassing it. Empirical data is still needed to test this hypothesis.

#### **b. This verb collapses the meaning of the two verbs it substitutes for, by bringing them near. This eludes the differentiated moral responsibility assigned to killing and dying.**
The ambiguity in _unalive_’s transitivity brings near “to kill” and “to die”. This broader way to refer to death shifts speakers’ attention to what is common to both of these verbs, that is to say, the outcome: a dead person. It shifts speakers’ attention away from the moral agent who can be held responsible for the outcome, unlike “to kill”. Making more salient the outcome might come at the cost of making less salient agency and differentiated moral responsibility assigned to killing and dying. Thus, _unalive_, when used transitively, and “kill” would have the same extensional extension but not the same intensional extension. These two ways of framing the same phenomenon would not be equivalent, as they might encourage speakers to make different choices (Fisher, S. A., & Mandel, D. R. (2021)). Choosing to describe the same phenomenon as an instance of _unaliving_ or of “killing” would not be indifferent, as the former would insist on the outcome while the latter would insist on the action which led to it.

Moreover, while “to kill” or “to die” are effectively replaced, words designating moral agents such as “killer” remain taboo on TikTok while no alternative is available. M.Moskal and N.Supernak do mention that “a suffixed form of _unalive_ is also found in _serial unaliver_ for _serial killer_” (Marlena Moskal & Natalia Supernak (2026)), but empirical studies would be needed to measure its actual occurrences. This linguistic gap makes it difficult to refer to actual moral agents who could be condemned for inflicting death, as users are forced upon awkward periphrases such as “the person who unalived someone”.

### **2.** **Its apparent composition** **raises** **the question** **of** **its antonym**

#### **a.** _To un+alive_ : what is unalive the antonym of?
While “to kill” and “to die” are not obviously composed, _to unalive_ is a verbed adjective whose composition is apparent. As M.Moskal and N.Supernak observe, “The perceived semantic transparency of this form is achieved by adding the prefix _-un_ to a well-entrenched lexical unit _alive_.”((Marlena Moskal & Natalia Supernak (2026)). The prefix looks like it was artificially added to a previously existing verb. _Unalive_ seems to be an antonym : TikTok users might try to build the verb it originates from by removing its prefix.
While “to alive” doesn’t exist, “alive” having not yet been verbed, one can think of two candidates for verbs _to unalive_ is built from: “To be alive” or “To come alive”.
#### **b. _To unalive_ as a negative action**
If _unalive_ is perceived as the antonym of “to be alive” or “to come alive”, it makes available a new conceptualization of death as either the absence of life, in the first case, or the absence of birth, in the second case. Either way, it allows to conceive death as a negation, as the undoing of something which has been done, thus going back to an initial state which has been disturbed by life or birth.

While “to kill” and “to die” appear to be positive actions as they are morphologically affirmative, the composition of _unalive_ (its apparent privative morphology) licenses the interpretation that it is not a positive action but the mere undoing of something. This matters, because a negative action might be more difficult to condemn morally : although duty to rescue exists and condemns inaction, suggesting that not engaging actively in a situation is not morally neutral, interfering is generally deemed more morally engaging than not. _To_ _unalive_ would then less need to be justified than “to kill”, as it would be considered to be less of an action.
### **3. To what extent does it qualify as significant conceptual change ?**
#### **a. A lexical change does not imply a conceptual one, but it makes it available**
A word’s apparent composition does not necessarily affect its meaning at all : for example, it would be absurd to claim that _to understand_ means to stay under something because it is composed of under + stand. Etymologies often don’t come to speakers’ mind after lexicalization.

However, _unalive_ has not yet been lexicalized since it has emerged recently (the first occurrence is dated from 2013). Consequently, it remains strange and its apparent composition might strike speakers coming across it for the first few times. This surprise might enable them to think about this term’s meaning, to reflect on the fact that it seems to be a negative action, and thus to conceptualize death otherwise. Without psycholinguistic evidence, this remains a hypothetical claim.
#### **b. How significant would this conceptual change be ? One illustration of the potential philosophical reach of this new conceptualization of death**
The adjectives “killed” and “dead” can be derived from the verbs “to kill” and “to die”. These predicates can only be attributed to beings who are not alive at the moment and who have previously lived. In this sense, death concerns beings who are not alive _anymore_. However, the adjective “unalive” derived from _to unalive_ has a broader extension, if it is understood as merely non-alive, like its morphology allows. It can be attributed to beings who are not alive, regardless of having lived at all or not. In this new sense, death concerns beings who are simply not alive at all.

In the first case, discriminating, from the set of not alive beings, the ones who have lived before, makes available justification for a differentiated treatment of beings who do not (yet) live and beings who do not live (anymore). One can ground a differentiated treatment on the fact that only the latter qualify as “dead”. The adjectives usually used to refer to death (“dead” and “killed”) invite speakers to think as if before coming to life, beings are not alive, whereas after coming to life and dying, they are positively dead. In this interpretation, the mere fact of coming to life would determine what could be said of a being, as even after ceasing to be alive it would not merely be “unalive” but rather “dead”. Beings would either be not alive (before life), alive (during life) or dead (after life).
In the second case, every being not alive, regardless of having lived or not, could be qualified as “unalive”. Death would not intrinsically differ from the prenatal state where one does not (yet) live. This vision negates the weight of past experience, as who has not yet come to life and who is deceased are both the same, i.e., unalive. Beings would be either alive (during life) or unalive (before and after life).

I hope to have shown that this term of algospeak makes available a conceptual change. Given that it was voluntarily invented, was coining it an instance of conceptual engineering?
In the second section of this article, I will explore the line between conceptual change and conceptual engineering, and ask at what conditions could _unalive_ be described as the latter.
## **II. Can _unalive_ be an instance of conceptual engineering ?**

### **1. At what conditions does conceptual change becomes conceptual engineering ?**
Conceptual engineering can be defined as “the project of designing, evaluating, and implementing concepts”. A concept can be designed through different-word linguistic engineering, which can be brought near to heteronymous conceptual-engineering (Chalmers, D. (2020).). But what does it mean to have the project of designing a concept?

According to Isaac, conceptual engineering relies on the premises that our concepts shape our worldview ; it then deals with our conceptual apparatuses in a prescriptive way. Concept implementation is guided by considerations about the cognitive optimization of our conceptual apparatuses. Concept-engineers distinguish themselves from concept-analysts: while the latter focus on what given concepts are, the former approach concepts as cognitive devices which have functions. They evaluate what they do and ask whether we should have such devices (Isaac, M. G. (2025), 2.2). Practical implementation is thus guided by an evaluative and normative method.

The sole word “death” seems unsatisfying, since it is unclear whether it refers to an event or a process, and since its ordinary use often clashes with its use in biology. As Gert et al. Argue, “It is both possible and preferable to use new precisely defined technical terms or phrases, for example, “in a persistent vegetative state,” in order to devise a policy for when it is morally acceptable to discontinue any life support, retrieve organs, and so forth. To use the same term, “death,” for all of these different stages is to invite confusion, mistrust, and abuse.” (Gert, Bernard, Culver, C, Danner Clouser, K (2006)). Implementing a new phrasing, such as _unalive_, makes room for much needed conceptual distinctions and could have a proper utility. For example, it could relate to death _as an outcome_, whereas “to kill” or “to die” could relate to death _as a process_. A conceptual engineer could legitimately investigate the words we use to talk about death and, with the intention of optimizing them, come up with _unalive_. _U__nalive_ can effectively serve a philosophical function, as it allows to make useful conceptual distinctions. But this seems to be contingent rather than intentional, since the term was not coined for this purpose.

### **2. Had users the project of designing a new concept ?**
#### **a. Users were constrained into coining this new term**
It is blatant that algospeakers’ intentions are not the ones concept-engineers ought to have, as they have coined _unalive_ under algorithmic constraints: its main purpose is to bypass censorship policies, by addressing a taboo topic through x-phemisms. Users engage in algospeak mainly as a strategic response against censorship and not because they want to optimize our conceptual apparatuses : they feel forced to shift their linguistic practice and their lexical innovation might entail conceptual changes. If they intentionally design new words, the design of concept feels forced rather than spontaneous. It is unrealistic to assume that users have consciously and voluntarily concept-engineered when they have invented _unalive_. In fact, it is possible that they did not think about its plausible conceptual consequences. Rather, _unalive_ has emerged out of pragmatic considerations and it was strongly constrained by TikTok’s actual censorship or collective beliefs about risks of being censored, that is, algorithmic folklore.

Then, if, as I have argued, _unalive_ allows an interesting re-conceptualization of death, it is merely contingent. One could argue that it qualifies as mere conceptual change rather than as concept-engineering, because it lacks intention. But does being constrained to shift their linguistic practices really impede users from being intentional ?

#### **b. A lexical innovation made with constrained intentionality. What about the conceptual innovation?**
TikTok users have some latitude to evade censorship. There exist many ways to x-phemistically refer to taboo topics, as M.Moskal and N.Supernak show by presenting the many euphemism formation mechanisms at work in algospeak. Users could have come up with many different ways to talk about death : the word _unalive_ was intentionally selected and not purely built out of constraint. It might have been preferred to alternatives because of the humorous effect it produces. This consideration reminds that one word might be used to pursue more than one goal. _Unalive_ is used to evade risks of being censored and to produce a humorous effect. One might then say that users coined _unalive_ with constrained intentionality. Could re-designing the concept of death have been another purpose of its emergence? If so, the conceptual change would be in part intentional, too, and it could be considered an instance of conceptual engineering.
Can reflecting on the concept of death be one of its function, when using it? If users probably did not coin it with the specific intention to reflect on death’s meaning or to convey it more accurately, it is nonetheless one of its possible function. Empirical data would be precious here to investigate how speakers feel about this word when they encounter it or when they decide to use it.

### **3. Conceptual engineering without conceptual engineer**

#### **a. _Unalive_ as a negotiation between the censoring platform, providing the necessary conditions, and its censored users, still wanting to discuss death despite perceived or actual algorithmic constraints**
If the concept of death provided by _unalive_ was deemed valuable, who could claim to be its author, given that it was produced under algorithmic constraints? To put it more simply: if it is concept-engineering, who is the concept-engineer? No conventional engineer is to be found. The authorship could not be attributed to an individual user, as the term only become part of algospeak once it is accepted by a community of users. But it could not be entirely attributed to a community of users either, since they have been constrained to adopt this term to bypass censorship. Finally, it could not be attributed entirely to TikTok as a platform, because although this term would not have existed without TikTok’s moderation policies, many other x-phemisms could have emerged, and have actually emerged, using different formation mechanisms. Moreover, it is not only TikTok’s actual censorship who caused this term to emerge, but rather the collective belief that death related words were taboo and that videos mentioning it would be censored. While the question of algospeak’s authorship remains open, it appears intrinsically negotiated between the censoring platform, the collective beliefs about censorship on the platform, and censored users.
#### **b. What function might conceptual engineering resulting of users’ responses to TikTok’s perceived censorship serve?**
Reflecting on the conditions of emergence of concepts through foucaultian genealogy was proposed as an instrument for Carnapian explication (Dutilh Novaes, C. (2025)), a process at the core of concept-engineering (Brun, G. (2016)). By showing _how_ the present is contingently made, the genealogical method has an explanatory and diagnostic function and is the basis for reconstruction (Koopman, C. (2013)). Genealogical investigation of concepts is thus a necessary step to evaluate them, point out their potential deficiency and engineer them. However, genealogical inquiry is also useful to reflect on the conditions of emergence of the result of conceptual-engineering. Keeping in mind the context of algorithmic governance which yields algospeak is fundamental to further evaluate its conceptual propositions, such as the one _unalive_ makes available. Algospeak appears as a response to perceived TikTok’s moderation policy, which depends both on algorithmic folklore and on the moral and intentional agents responsible for the actual moderation policy. Those agents, through the control of the platform’s moderation policies, hand-in-hand with users spreading algorithmic folklore, ultimately have a great power: by making some death-related-words taboo, they discourage users to employ them and make some conceptualizations of death less available. Through algospeak, TikTok users are provided access to the new conceptualization of death that _unalive_ makes available, but the other conceptualization of death made available by words such as “to kill” or “to die” is less accessible. Censoring words can be a strategy to mitigate some ways of thinking about death, by making the conceptualizations which depends on these words less available through the language authorized on the platform. Evaluating a concept involves checking that it fulfills its function, and concept-engineering is successful if the outcome concept better fulfills its function (Thomasson, A. L. (2020).). If the engineering of the concept of death has really been made possible through a term provided by algospeak, and given that algospeak is a direct answer to TikTok’s perceived censorship policy, one can legitimately ask what function is better fulfilled when death is thought about as a negation rather than as a positive event or state. Moreover, one can ask if this function is legitimate and if it serves any of TikTok’s private purposes.

## Conclusion
To conclude, TikTok’s censorship policy, or what users collectively perceive as such, has instigated algospeak as an answer to algorithmic constraints. I have argued that _unalive_, an instance of algospeak, is a lexical innovation which makes room for conceptual change. It makes more salient the outcome and might shift speakers’ attention away from agency. It might license the conception of death as the mere negation of life. Moreover, and given that this lexical innovation is partly intentional, although emerging in a constrained environment, it could be seen as an instance of collective conceptual engineering, by reflecting on old concepts and designing words to articulate them otherwise, thereby necessarily changing their meaning in turn. This encourages to reconsider the idea that concept-engineering is individual, wholly intentional and voluntary. If algorithms can indirectly change our conceptual apparatuses, it becomes urgent to reflect on the concepts algorithms (or people implementing them) might try to enforce and why.
